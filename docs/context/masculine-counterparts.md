# Masculine counterparts for the Word Hoard rooms

Context file for the Word Hoard threads. Compiled 2026-10-06. Companion data: `masculine-counterparts.json` in this folder.

**What this is.** For each AO room (Poetry, Folk Songs, Hymns, Art, Composers, Year 0) plus a cross-room Study, the best public-domain works by old-world men to sit beside the AO selections: anthologies, ballad and song collections, hymns, pictures, composer studies, and essays on manhood, vocation and chivalry. Each entry has a checked source link, why it can be trusted, and what Lewis or Chesterton actually said, with inference labelled as inference.

**Verification levels used on every quote.**
- `full-text`: Matched word for word against the complete public-domain text
- `source-fetched`: Copied from the source page or book by a researcher; not re-matched against a complete text
- `secondary`: Taken from a reliable secondary page quoting the original; the original book was not opened

**Rules for using this file.** Quote only what is printed here, exactly as printed. A Lewis or Chesterton connection marked `inferred` is our reasoning, not his words, and must never be presented as his. Lewis texts (he died 1963) are in US copyright; use the short Lewis lines here as citations, not as hosted Mnemonicon text.

## Cautions to carry into every room

- Lewis on hymns: he said he had disliked hymns as "fifth-rate poems set to sixth-rate music" ("Answers to Questions on Christianity", 1944, in *God in the Dock*), then told how a humble old believer cured his pride. Source level: secondary (two blogs quoting the book agree). Do not claim Lewis endorsed the hymn room; Bunyan is the safe Lewis link there.
- Chesterton on success and hero worship: he attacked success-worship books ("The Fallacy of Success", 1908, full-text) and warned that a creed "which set out to create conquerors would only corrupt soldiers" (on the Carlyleans, 1913, full-text). So leave out Samuel Smiles; use Carlyle only with care. No Chesterton line naming Smiles was found.
- Kipling is a mixed case for Chesterton: praise for "the romance of the division of labour and the discipline of all the trades", and the charge "He admires England, but he does not love her" (Heretics, 1905, both full-text).
- Gautier's sixth commandment of chivalry ("war against the Infidel... without mercy") needs framing for children.
- Wagner's myth is the root of Lewis's "Northernness", but Wagner's own writings carry known antisemitism. Present the myth and the Rackham pictures, not the man.
- Newbolt's "play the game" school ethos: no Lewis statement found; Lewis disliked his own public school (inferred caution only). Keep Newbolt secondary.
- Johnson's "Every man thinks meanly of himself for not having been a soldier" (Boswell, 1778) is NOT yet verified against the text. Do not use it until checked.

## Poetry: Heroic, adventurous and vocational verse

### ★ W. E. Henley (ed.): Lyra Heroica: A Book of Verse for Boys

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 19316](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19316)
- **Why trust him:** A late-Victorian anthology built for boys from the ballads, Shakespeare, Drayton, Campbell, Macaulay, Tennyson and others. Henley's preface states the purpose plainly. The text was fetched and confirmed: it includes Horatius, Chevy Chase, Sir Patrick Spens, Ye Mariners of England, and Tennyson's 'A Ballad of the Fleet' (The Revenge).
- **Fit:** The direct boys' counterpart to an Ambleside poetry anthology. Its stated aim is to teach courage, devotion and patriotism through verse.
- **Lewis (our inference, not his words):** No direct statement by Lewis about Lyra Heroica was found. INFERRED: the book is built from the ballad and heroic tradition (Chevy Chase, Macaulay, Scott) that Lewis valued. — none found
- **Chesterton (our inference, not his words):** No direct statement by Chesterton about Lyra Heroica was found. INFERRED: its content overlaps with the Macaulay and Scott romance he praised (see those entries). — none found
- **Rights:** Published 1891 and Henley died 1903, so it is PD in the US. The Gutenberg text is from a 1920 reprint.

### ★ G. K. Chesterton: The Ballad of the White Horse (1911); 'Lepanto' (in Poems, 1915)

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 1719](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1719); [Gutenberg 31184](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31184)
- **Why trust him:** Chesterton's own heroic epic about Alfred against the Danes, and his poem on Don John of Austria at Lepanto. Lewis is documented quoting the Ballad with admiration.
- **Fit:** These poems teach Christian courage without hope of easy victory: Alfred's 'joy without a cause'. It is the best single fit for this room.
- **Lewis (secondary source):** "in those quaking days after the fall of France [...] [we] found ourselves quoting to one another stanza after stanza of the Ballad. There was nothing else to say". Lewis also defended the poem as 'permanent and dateless'. This is SECONDARY: quoted by Nicholas Milne, Mythlore 35.1 (2016), from Lewis's 'Period Criticism' (1946; repr. Of This and Other Worlds, 1982, pp. 147-52). The Lewis original was not fetched. — Milne, 'Chesterton's Ballad of the White Horse: From Conception to Critical Reception', Mythlore 35.1 (2016), https://dc.swosu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1062&context=mythlore
- **Lewis (secondary source):** "Here and there it achieves the heroic, the rarest quality in modern literature". Lewis said this in conversation, as recalled by George Sayer. It is SECONDARY: quoted in the same Milne Mythlore article, citing Sayer. — Milne, Mythlore 35.1 (2016), same URL
- **Rights:** Both are pre-1930 publications, so they are PD in the US. The Gutenberg header for Poems gives a 1916 edition.

### Thomas Babington Macaulay: Lays of Ancient Rome (Horatius)

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 847](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/847)
- **Why trust him:** A standard schoolroom recitation piece for 150 years, and Henley reprinted Horatius in Lyra Heroica. Chesterton praised Macaulay's 'abstract passion for history' and his 'poetry'.
- **Fit:** Horatius at the bridge is the archetype of the man who holds the gap for his city. It is ideal for boys' memory work.
- **Chesterton (checked in his own text):** His noble enduring quality in our literature is this: that he truly had an abstract passion for history; a warm, poetic and sincere enthusiasm for great things as such; an ardour and appetite for great books, great battles, great cities, great men. He felt and used names like trumpets. — The Victorian Age in Literature, 1913, ch. 1, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/18639/pg18639.txt (cross-checked in the full text from the GITenberg mirror)
- **Chesterton (critical; checked in his own text):** There were two Macaulays, a rational Macaulay who was generally wrong, and a romantic Macaulay who was almost invariably right. — The Victorian Age in Literature, 1913, Gutenberg 18639
- **Lewis (our inference, not his words):** No statement by Lewis was found. INFERRED only. — none found
- **Rights:** Published 1842 and the author died 1859, so it is PD.

### Rudyard Kipling: Barrack-Room Ballads (1892); 'If—' in Rewards and Fairies (1910)

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 2819](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2819); [Gutenberg 556](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/556)
- **Why trust him:** These are the most widely memorised poems of duty in English. Chesterton gave them a careful two-sided judgment in Heretics. Lewis wrote a whole essay on Kipling.
- **Fit:** Kipling is the poet of the trades and of discipline: 'the romance of the division of labour'. That makes him the vocational heart of the room.
- **Chesterton (checked in his own text):** The real poetry, the "true romance" which Mr. Kipling has taught, is the romance of the division of labour and the discipline of all the trades. He sings the arts of peace much more accurately than the arts of war. And his main contention is vital and valuable. — Heretics, 1905, ch. 'On Mr. Rudyard Kipling and Making the World Small', https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/470/pg470.txt (full text grep-checked)
- **Chesterton (critical; checked in his own text):** The great gap in his mind is what may be roughly called the lack of patriotism--that is to say, he lacks altogether the faculty of attaching himself to any cause or community finally and tragically; for all finality must be tragic. He admires England, but he does not love her; for we admire things with reasons, but love them without reasons. — Heretics, 1905, same chapter, Gutenberg 470
- **Lewis (the work is confirmed to exist):** Lewis wrote the essay 'Kipling's World', reprinted in They Asked for a Paper (1962) and Selected Literary Essays (1969). Its existence was confirmed from the lewisiana.nl essay bibliography, which dates first publication 1944 (it is usually cited as Literature and Life, 1948). The text was not fetched. His view, from memory and UNVERIFIED: he admired Kipling's celebration of work and craft but was uneasy with the 'inner ring' and professional cult. — https://lewisiana.nl/cslessays/index.htm; fadedpage They Asked for a Paper https://fadedpage.com/books/20150423/html.php
- **Rights:** Barrack-Room Ballads (1892) and Rewards and Fairies (1910) were published before 1931, so they are PD in the US. Use pre-1931 poems only.

### Sir Walter Scott: Marmion (1808); The Lay of the Last Minstrel (1805), Canto VI 'Breathes there the man'

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 4010](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4010); [poets.org (Academy of American Poets), Lay Canto VI extract](https://poets.org/anthology/sir-walter-scott)
- **Why trust him:** Scott was the father of chivalric romance in English. Chesterton wrote a whole essay defending him, and Lewis gave a Scott address.
- **Fit:** Border chivalry and love of one's own land. 'Breathes there the man' is a classic recitation.
- **Chesterton (checked in his own text):** In truth, one of Scott's most splendid traits is his difficulty, or rather incapacity, for despising any of his characters. He did not scorn the most revolting miscreant as the realist of to-day commonly scorns his own hero. Though his soul may be in rags, every man of Scott can speak like a king. — Twelve Types, 1902, 'The Position of Sir Walter Scott', Gutenberg 12491 https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12491 (WebFetch truncates before this chapter; verified in the full text from the GITenberg mirror raw.githubusercontent.com/GITenberg/Twelve-Types_12491/master/12491.txt)
- **Chesterton (checked in his own text):** Romance, indeed, does not consist by any means so much in experiencing adventures as in being ready for them. — Twelve Types, 1902, same essay, same source
- **Lewis (the work is confirmed to exist):** Lewis wrote the essay 'Sir Walter Scott' (first published 1956; reprinted in They Asked for a Paper, 1962, and Selected Literary Essays, 1969). Its existence was confirmed via the lewisiana.nl bibliography. The text was not fetched. — https://lewisiana.nl/cslessays/index.htm
- **Rights:** Published 1805/1808 and Scott died 1832, so it is PD. The Gutenberg Lay of the Last Minstrel ID could not be found. Marmion 4010 (Morley ed.) was confirmed. The 'Breathes there' text comes from poets.org, not Gutenberg.

### Sir Henry Newbolt: Admirals All (1897); in Collected Poems 1897-1907 (Drake's Drum, Vitaï Lampada)

### Beowulf, tr. Francis B. Gummere; William Morris, Sigurd the Volsung: Beowulf (Gummere tr., 1909/1910 Harvard Classics); The Story of Sigurd the Volsung and the Fall of the Niblungs (1876)

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 981](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/981); [Gutenberg 18328](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/18328)
- **Why trust him:** These are the old Northern heroic epics in Victorian/Edwardian verse. Morris was a formative author for Lewis. Gummere's translation is the scholarly standard of its day.
- **Fit:** This is 'Northernness': the hero facing monster and doom. Lewis named it as the root of his imagination.
- **Lewis (the work is confirmed to exist):** Lewis wrote the essay 'William Morris' (first published 1937; reprinted in Rehabilitations, 1939, and Selected Literary Essays, 1969). Its existence was confirmed via the lewisiana.nl bibliography. The text was not fetched (Rehabilitations is not on fadedpage). — https://lewisiana.nl/cslessays/index.htm
- **Chesterton (checked in his own text):** The manner in which Morris carried out his crusade may, considering the circumstances, be called triumphant. — Twelve Types, 1902, 'William Morris and His School', Gutenberg 12491 (grep-verified in full text)
- **Chesterton (critical; checked in his own text):** But herein, indeed, lay Morris's deepest claim to the name of a great reformer: that he left his work incomplete. — Twelve Types, 1902, same essay
- **Lewis (our inference, not his words):** Beowulf: no specific Lewis statement was fetched. INFERRED from his well-known love of Norse and Old English 'Northernness'. — none fetched
- **Rights:** Sigurd was published 1876 and Morris died 1896, so it is PD. Gummere died 1919 and the translation is pre-1930, so it is PD. Gutenberg 13486 is a different Volsung text, so use 18328 for the poem.

## Folk Songs: Ballads, sea shanties and soldiers' songs

### ★ Thomas Percy (ed.; 1885 Wheatley edition on Gutenberg): Reliques of Ancient English Poetry

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 45939](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45939/pg45939.txt)
- **Why trust him:** An Anglican bishop (Dromore) whose collection rescued the old minstrel ballads and directly fed Scott, Wordsworth and the whole ballad revival.
- **Fit:** Opens with Chevy Chase - border warfare, vows, loyalty and valour - the archetypal English heroic ballad.
- **Chesterton (our inference, not his words):** INFERRED: no direct Chesterton statement on Percy found this session. His essay on Scott (verified) praises the romance Scott drew from this ballad tradition. — n/a
- **Lewis (our inference, not his words):** INFERRED: none found. Lewis's admiration for Sidney (who praised the Percy-Douglas song) is well known but his remarks on the ballad were not confirmed this session. — n/a
- **Rights:** 1765; Percy d.1811; Wheatley ed. 1876-86, Wheatley d.1917. PD in US.

### ★ Richard Runciman Terry (ed.), foreword by Sir Walter Runciman: The Shanty Book, Part I: Sailor Shanties

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 20774](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20774/pg20774.txt)
- **Why trust him:** ADDED. Terry, director of music at Westminster Cathedral and restorer of Tudor church music, learned these shanties as a boy from sailors; foreword by a shipowner who went to sea as a boy.
- **Fit:** Clear introduction 'What a shanty is' plus words and tunes (Shenandoah etc.) - ready-made work-song memory pieces.
- **Chesterton (our inference, not his words):** None found; inferred - Terry was a prominent Catholic musician of Chesterton's circle era. — n/a
- **Rights:** Published 1921, before 1931, so PD in the US.

### Sir Walter Scott (ed.): Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border

- **Source:** [Gutenberg 12742](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12742)
- **Why trust him:** Scott collected Border ballads by field-work and manuscript; Chesterton defended him as a master of romance.
- **Fit:** Reivers, raids, loyal clansmen and sea-disaster ballads (Sir Patrick Spens) - the Border's soldier and fighting songs.
- **Chesterton (checked in his own text):** "In the selection of these situations which catch the spirit of romance as in a net, Scott has never been equalled or even approached." — Twelve Types, 'The Position of Sir Walter Scott', 1902, https://www.online-literature.com/chesterton/twelve-types/12/ (also Gutenberg 12491, but that file truncated before the essay in fetch)
- **Chesterton (checked in his own text):** "Scott takes his heroes and villains seriously, which is, after all, the way that heroes and villains take themselves--especially villains." — Twelve Types, 1902, https://www.online-literature.com/chesterton/twelve-types/12/
- **Lewis (our inference, not his words):** INFERRED: Lewis read and reread Scott (he lectured on Scott in 'Sir Walter Scott', in They Asked for a Paper, 1962) - essay existence not confirmed by fetch this session. — They Asked for a Paper, 1962 (not fetched)
- **Rights:** 1802-03; Scott d.1832. PD. Note: Chesterton essay contains no mention of the ballads/Border specifically - the regard is for Scott as romancer.
