# The Iliad

> Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought
> countless ills upon the Achaeans.
> <small>`iliad-butler:1.1`</small>

## Opening

Look at the hands.

The *Iliad* is twenty-four books of hands. Hands that grip spears. Hands
that pour wine to gods who aren't listening. Hands that strip the armor off
a dead man while his friends try to drag him away by the feet. And there are
two hands in particular, Achilles' hands, which do more killing in this poem
than any others. When the poem is almost over, an old king crosses the enemy
lines at night, kneels in the dirt, and kisses them.

The first time you read it, that scene stops you cold. It should. It's one of
the three or four greatest scenes ever written, and a blind Greek poet wrote
it at least seven hundred years before Christ.

Which raises the question this chapter exists to answer. Why should a
Christian spend weeks with a poem about pagans who worship adulterous gods,
butcher prisoners, and think the best thing a man can leave behind is a
famous name? The short answer is that the *Iliad* is the most honest account
ever given of what man is without grace. Homer doesn't flatter his heroes, he
doesn't lie about war, and he doesn't pretend the gods will fix it. He shows
you the whole machinery of pride, rage, glory and grief running at full
speed, and then, in the last book, he shows you it breaking down, and a
light getting in through the crack.

You can't understand the Gospel's answer until you've felt the question. Homer
makes you feel it.

## General Information

### Author and context

We don't know who Homer was. The Greeks themselves didn't know. They said he
was blind and from Ionia, on the coast of what is now Turkey, and they
treated his two poems, the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*, as the foundation of
everything else they knew. Most scholars now date the poems to somewhere in
the eighth or seventh century BC. That puts them roughly in the time of the
prophets Isaiah and Hosea.

The poems grew out of a tradition of oral singing. Bards performed long
stories from memory, built out of stock phrases ("swift-footed Achilles,"
"Hector tamer of horses") that let a singer compose in meter as he went.
You'll notice the repetitions. They aren't padding. They're the scaffolding
of a poem made to be heard.

The war the poem describes, a Greek siege of Troy (Ilium) to win back Helen
from the Trojan prince Paris, was already old legend when Homer sang it. The
poem doesn't tell the whole war. It covers a few weeks in the tenth year, and
it tells the story of one man's anger.

**A note on names.** Butler, following the fashion of his day, calls the gods
by their Latin names: Jove for Zeus, Juno for Hera, Minerva for Athena, Mars
for Ares, Vulcan for Hephaestus. The Greeks are variously called Achaeans,
Argives and Danaans. They're all the same people.

### Significance

Every epic after this one is answering it. Virgil's *Aeneid* (chapter 18)
rewrites it for Rome. Beowulf, Roland, Dante and Milton are all in some sense
in conversation with it. Alexander the Great slept with a copy under his
pillow. For two and a half thousand years, "hero" meant, more or less, a man
like Achilles. To read the *Iliad* is to meet the original of that idea
before Christianity got its hands on it. That's why reading it well is a
lesson in what Christianity changed.

### The map of the poem

- **Books 1–4.** Agamemnon, commander of the Greeks, takes Achilles' prize
  woman Briseis to replace a captive he has been forced to give back. Achilles,
  the best warrior alive, withdraws from the fighting and asks his goddess
  mother Thetis to get Jove to let the Greeks lose until they come begging.
- **Books 5–8.** The war goes on without him. Diomedes wounds gods. Hector,
  Troy's champion, goes home and says goodbye to his wife and son. Jove tips
  the scales toward the Trojans.
- **Book 9.** An embassy offers Achilles enormous gifts to return. He refuses.
- **Books 10–15.** The Trojans break through the wall and reach the ships.
- **Book 16.** Achilles' closest friend Patroclus goes into battle in
  Achilles' armor and is killed by Hector.
- **Books 17–21.** Achilles' grief turns into a new and worse rage. His
  mother brings new armor forged by Vulcan, with the famous shield. He
  slaughters Trojans until the river itself fights him.
- **Book 22.** Achilles kills Hector and drags the body behind his chariot.
- **Books 23–24.** Patroclus is buried with games. Priam, Hector's father,
  comes by night to ransom his son's body. Achilles gives it up. The poem ends
  with Hector's funeral.

## Worldview: The Hands of Achilles

Start with the strongest case for Achilles, because he deserves one.

The world of the *Iliad* is short, violent and dark. Nobody in it expects
anything good after death: the dead go down to Hades as witless shadows. The
gods are powerful, quarrelsome, and not especially interested in justice.
Given all that, what's left for a man to live for? Achilles' answer is a
name. If death is certain and the afterlife is a gray nothing, then the only
thing a man can make that outlasts him is the memory of what he did. So he
should do something worth remembering, and do it at whatever cost. Achilles
knows the price exactly. His mother has told him:

> If I stay here and fight, I shall not return alive but my name will live
> for ever: whereas if I go home my name will die, but it will be long ere
> death shall take me.
> <small>`iliad-butler:9.334`</small>

That is not a savage's code. It is a serious, clear-eyed answer to a serious
question, and most of what we still admire in soldiers comes out of it:
courage in the face of certain death, loyalty to comrades, contempt for the
coward who saves his skin. A Christian who sneers at Achilles hasn't
understood him. Homer's heroes are what the best men look like when they
have been told the truth about death but not the whole truth.

Now look at what that code actually produces.

It produces the first line of the poem. The *anger* of Achilles "brought
countless ills upon the Achaeans". It killed his own side. Achilles'
wounded pride is worth more to him than the lives of the men he fights
beside, and he says so. He asks the gods to let them be slaughtered at their
ships, so that Agamemnon will learn what he has lost. The glory code has no
way to stop this, because glory is a zero-sum good. There's only so much of
it to go around, and every man's honor is measured against every other
man's. Agamemnon takes Briseis not because he wants her, but because the
commander cannot be seen to lose a prize while a subordinate keeps his. The
whole quarrel is two men keeping score.

Scripture has a sentence for it: *the wrath of man worketh not the
righteousness of God* (`kjv:Jas.1.20`). And another, which reads like a
direct answer to Achilles: *he that is slow to anger is better than the
mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city*
(`kjv:Prov.16.32`). Achilles could take any city on earth. He cannot rule his
own spirit for a single afternoon.

Then Patroclus dies, and the anger does not end. It changes direction. The
Achilles who comes back to the war in book 19 is not a hero rediscovering his
duty. He's a man who has stopped caring whether he lives, and has become an
engine for making other men dead. He cuts the throats of twelve Trojan
prisoners at Patroclus' pyre. He kills Hector, and then he does the thing
the whole ancient world regarded as unspeakable. He refuses the body burial,
drags it in the dust behind his chariot, and promises it to the dogs.

Here the poem touches something the Bible also cares about fiercely. When
Goliath wants to terrify David, he says *I will give thy flesh unto the fowls
of the air, and to the beasts of the field* (`kjv:1Sam.17.44`). It's the same
threat that the first lines of the *Iliad* use for the
fruit of Achilles' anger: heroes made "a prey to dogs and vultures." Israel's
law required even an executed criminal to be buried the same day
(`kjv:Deut.21.23`). And Rizpah, a grieving mother, guarded the bodies of her
sons day and night from birds and beasts, until King David was shamed into
burying them properly (`kjv:2Sam.21.10`). The dignity of the human body, even
a dead one, even an enemy's, is written deep in both books. In Achilles, the
glory code has reached the place where it cannot tell a man from meat.

And that is where the hands come in.

In book 24, Priam, the old king of Troy, loads a wagon with treasure and
drives by night into the Greek camp. The gods help him get there, but when
he walks into Achilles' hut he is on his own:

> King Priam entered without their seeing him, and going right up to
> Achilles he clasped his knees and kissed the dread murderous hands that
> had slain so many of his sons.
> <small>`iliad-butler:24.468`</small>

Then he speaks. He doesn't threaten Achilles or flatter him. He asks him to
remember his own father:

> Fear, O Achilles, the wrath of heaven; think on your own father and have
> compassion upon me, who am the more pitiable, for I have steeled myself as
> no man yet has ever steeled himself before me, and have raised to my lips
> the hand of him who slew my son.
> <small>`iliad-butler:24.468`</small>

And Achilles weeps. The two enemies weep together, the old man for his son
and the young man for his father and his friend. Achilles gives back the
body, and even grants a truce for Hector's funeral. For one night,
the glory code is suspended by something older and deeper than itself.

It is the most Christian moment in any pagan book, and it's worth saying
exactly why, and exactly where it stops short.

It's Christian because it shows that the way out of the cycle of vengeance
is not a stronger warrior, but a humbled one. Priam wins back his son by
doing the thing no hero would ever do: kneeling to the man who has wronged
him. *Love your enemies*, Jesus says, *do good to them that hate you*
(`kjv:Matt.5.44`). Homer doesn't have that command, but he has seen, as if at
a great distance, the shape of the thing it commands.

It stops short in three places.

First, it doesn't last. Achilles warns Priam not to provoke him, because he
can feel the rage still waiting under the surface. The truce lasts only as long as
the funeral. Everyone knows Troy will burn anyway.

Second, it has no ground to stand on. Achilles' own explanation of why men
suffer is the image of the two urns on the floor of Jove's palace, one full
of evil gifts and one of good, from which the god hands out mixed or purely
evil lots as he pleases (`iliad-butler:24.468`). That is fate, not
providence. It sounds a little like Job asking *shall we receive good at the
hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?* (`kjv:Job.2.10`), except that
Job's God is good, knows Job's name, and answers him from the whirlwind.
Jove's urns answer nobody.

Third, and most important, somebody still has to beg for the body. In the
*Iliad*, the father crosses into enemy territory to kiss the killer's hands.
The Gospels turn this inside out. When the Son lies dead, a disciple goes to
the ruler who condemned Him and *besought Pilate that he might take away the
body of Jesus* (`kjv:John.19.38`). But the body does not stay ransomed and
buried, because the Father did not come to kneel before the killers. He
came, in the Son, to be killed, and to rise, so that *through death he might
destroy him that had the power of death* (`kjv:Heb.2.14`). The hands that
matter at the end of the Christian story are not the hands that killed. They
are the hands that were pierced.

So read the *Iliad* the way you would listen to a brilliant man who has
thought harder about death than anyone around him, and has been honest
enough to say that his answer isn't enough. Admire Achilles. Grieve with
Hector. Take Priam's courage as a gift. And then close the book knowing that
the glory Achilles died for was the smaller glory, the one Babel was after
when it said *let us make us a name* (`kjv:Gen.11.4`), and that the greater
glory belongs to the One who *made himself of no reputation*
(`kjv:Phil.2.7`).

Homer got an old man to kiss the hands of his son's killer. Only God put
nails through His own.

## Sessions

### Session 1 · Books 1–4 · The Quarrel

**Before you read.** Watch what each man says he wants, and what he actually
fights for. Keep a list of every time someone mentions a prize, a gift or
honor.

**Comprehension**
1. Why does Agamemnon have to give up Chryseis, and what does he take in her
   place?
2. What stops Achilles from killing Agamemnon on the spot in the assembly?
3. What does Achilles ask Thetis to get from Jove?
4. Who fights the duel in book 3, and how does it end?

**Interpretive**
5. Is Achilles right to withdraw from the fighting? Argue from the text,
   not from what you think of him.
6. Nestor tries to make peace (book 1). Why does he fail?

**Cultural analysis.** Agamemnon and Achilles both treat honor as something
that can be taken away by someone else. Where do people today act as if
their worth is set by what others give or take from them?

**Biblical analysis.** Compare Achilles' anger with Saul's jealousy of David
(1 Sam. 18). What does each man think he has lost? Now read `kjv:Jas.1.20`
and `kjv:Prov.16.32`. What would it have looked like for Achilles to "rule
his spirit"?

**Application.** When you've been wronged by someone in authority, what's the
difference between an appeal and a withdrawal?

**Summa.** What is the anger of Achilles actually *about*?

### Session 2 · Books 5–8 · Men Like Gods, Gods Like Men

**Before you read.** Notice how the gods behave in battle. Ask yourself
whether you would want to be ruled by them.

**Comprehension**
1. Which gods does Diomedes wound, and with whose help?
2. Why does Hector go back into the city in book 6?
3. What happens between Glaucus and Diomedes when they meet on the field?
4. What does Jove do with his golden scales in book 8?

**Interpretive**
5. Hector says goodbye to Andromache and to his son Astyanax, who is
   frightened by his father's helmet. Why does Homer include the helmet?
6. Glaucus compares the generations of men to leaves (the memory passage).
   Does he mean it as despair, or as something else?

**Cultural analysis.** The Greek gods are stronger than men, but not better.
What happens to a culture whose gods are simply power?

**Biblical analysis.** Set Glaucus' leaves beside `kjv:Isa.40.6`–`8` and
`kjv:Ps.103.15`. Both say man is like grass or leaves. Where do they part
company? (Look at the end of Isa. 40:8.)

**Application.** Hector is the most admirable man in the poem: a husband, a
father, a defender of his city. What does he get right that Achilles gets
wrong?

**Summa.** What is the difference between a god you fear and a god you
trust?

### Session 3 · Books 9–12 · The Embassy

**Before you read.** Book 9 is the hinge of the poem. Read Achilles' reply
to Odysseus aloud, slowly.

**Comprehension**
1. List the gifts Agamemnon offers. Does he offer an apology?
2. Who are the three members of the embassy?
3. What two fates has Thetis told Achilles about?
4. What happens to the Greek wall?

**Interpretive**
5. Achilles says the gifts mean nothing to him. Is he telling the truth?
6. Why does Phoenix tell the story of Meleager?

**Cultural analysis.** Agamemnon tries to buy his way out of the quarrel.
Where do we try to settle moral debts with money?

**Biblical analysis.** Achilles chooses between a long life and a lasting
name. Read `kjv:Gen.11.4` and `kjv:Matt.16.26`. What does Scripture say a
name is worth, and who gives the lasting one?

**Application.** Achilles is too proud to take what he asked for once it's
offered on someone else's terms. When have you refused a good thing because
you didn't get to set the terms?

**Summa.** Why does Achilles say no?

### Session 4 · Books 13–17 · Patroclus

**Before you read.** Pay attention to Patroclus. He's quieter than the other
heroes. Ask why Achilles loves him.

**Comprehension**
1. What does Patroclus ask of Achilles, and what warning comes with the
   answer?
2. Achilles tells Patroclus to drive the Trojans from the ships and then
   come back. What does Patroclus do instead?
3. Which three figures bring about Patroclus' death?
4. What happens to Achilles' armor?

**Interpretive**
5. Is Achilles responsible for Patroclus' death? Make the strongest case
   for yes and for no.
6. Why does Homer describe the fight over Patroclus' body at such length
   (book 17)?

**Cultural analysis.** The heroes fight as hard for a corpse as for a
living man. What do they believe the body is? What do we believe?

**Biblical analysis.** Read David's lament for Jonathan (2 Sam. 1:17–27).
Compare it with Achilles' grief in book 18. What does each man do with his
sorrow?

**Application.** Patroclus goes into battle wearing someone else's armor.
What happens when you try to live in someone else's reputation?

**Summa.** Who killed Patroclus?

### Session 5 · Books 18–21 · The Shield and the River

**Before you read.** The description of the shield (book 18) is a whole
world in miniature. Make a sketch of it as you read.

**Comprehension**
1. What does Thetis do when she hears Achilles' cry?
2. Name four scenes on the shield.
3. How is the quarrel with Agamemnon finally settled?
4. Why does the river Scamander attack Achilles?

**Interpretive**
5. The shield shows a city at peace and a city at war, a wedding and a
   lawsuit, plowing and harvest and dancing. Why does Achilles carry *that*
   into his most savage fighting?
6. Lycaon begs Achilles for his life (book 21). Achilles answers:
   "Therefore, my friend, you too shall die. Why should you whine in this
   way? Patroclus fell, and he was a better man than you are." What has
   changed in Achilles since the beginning of the poem?

**Cultural analysis.** The shield puts the ordinary life of cities (courts,
weddings, harvests) at the center of the world, and war at the edge. Is
that true of Homer's world? Is it true of ours?

**Biblical analysis.** Read `kjv:Rom.12.19`. What is the difference between
vengeance and justice, and whose is each?

**Application.** Grief can turn to rage. What's the difference between
grieving well and grieving badly?

**Summa.** What does the shield know that Achilles doesn't?

### Session 6 · Books 22–24 · The Ransom

**Before you read.** Read Priam's visit to Achilles (book 24) twice: once
quickly, once aloud.

**Comprehension**
1. Why does Hector stay outside the gates when the other Trojans retreat?
2. How does Athena deceive Hector?
3. What does Achilles do to Hector's body, and how do the gods respond?
4. What does Priam ask of Achilles, and what does Achilles give him?

**Interpretive**
5. Hector's dying words warn Achilles of his own death. Why does Achilles
   answer, "Die; for my part I will accept my fate whensoever Jove and the
   other gods see fit to send it"?
6. Why does the poem end with Hector's funeral, not with Achilles' death or
   the fall of Troy?

**Cultural analysis.** For one night, Achilles and Priam see their enemy as
a father and a son. What makes that possible? Why doesn't it last?

**Biblical analysis.** Set Priam begging for Hector's body beside
`kjv:John.19.38` and `kjv:2Sam.21.10`. Then read `kjv:Heb.2.14`–`15`. Where
does the Gospel go that Homer can't?

**Application.** Priam kisses the hands of the man who killed his son.
Whose hands are you refusing to take?

**Summa.** What does Priam win?

## Recitation

Memorize Glaucus' answer to Diomedes (`iliad-butler:6.102`, Il. 6.146–149):

> Men come and go as leaves year by year upon the trees. Those of autumn
> the wind sheds upon the ground, but when spring returns the forest buds
> forth with fresh vines. Even so is it with the generations of mankind, the
> new spring up as the old are passing away.

Pair it with Isaiah 40:8: *The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the
word of our God shall stand for ever.*

## Writing

- **Essay.** "The *Iliad* is a poem about anger, but it is not an angry
  poem." Defend or attack this claim, with at least four quotations cited by
  unit id.
- **Creative.** Write the conversation between Hector and Andromache that
  might have happened if Hector had come home once more before his death.
  Keep Homer's manner: formal, direct, no modern slang.
- **Socratic debate.** Resolved: *Achilles was right to choose the short
  life and the lasting name.* Argue the affirmative, at full strength, as a
  Greek would have. Then argue the negative from Scripture.

## Threads

- **Back:** Gilgamesh (ch. 2) also grieves a dead friend and goes looking
  for immortality. Compare Gilgamesh's grief for Enkidu with Achilles' for
  Patroclus.
- **Forward:** the *Odyssey* (ch. 6), where the dead Achilles in the
  underworld reconsiders his choice (Od. 11.489ff). Also the *Aeneid*
  (ch. 18), which begins with Troy burning, and Beowulf (vol. II), a hero who
  dies for his people rather than for his name.
- **Across:** 1 and 2 Samuel (ch. 8). David, a warrior-king who also
  weeps for his enemies (2 Sam. 1; 18:33), is the Bible's answer to
  Achilles.
