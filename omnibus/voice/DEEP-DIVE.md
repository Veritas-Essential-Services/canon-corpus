# The House Voice: A Deep Dive

`VOICE.md` is the one-page card to keep open while writing. This is the
long form: where the voice comes from, how it works sentence by sentence,
where it fails, and how to tell the difference.

**On quotations.** Every quotation below was checked word for word against a
public-domain text: Chesterton's *Orthodoxy* (1908, PG #130), *Heretics*
(1905, PG #470) and *What's Wrong with the World* (1910, PG #1717); Pascal's
*Pensées* in W. F. Trotter's translation (PG #18269, fragments by
Brunschvicg number); and the KJV. The copyrighted and living influences are
described by their methods, never quoted and never imitated. Where I know an
influence only thinly, I say so.

---

## Part 1. What the voice is

In one sentence:

> **A glad man who has read everything, believes the Creed, and is not
> afraid of you, explaining an old book to someone he'd like to see become
> wise.**

Every word in that is doing work:

- **Glad.** The joy comes before the polemic and outlasts it. The voice
  laughs more than it scowls.
- **Has read everything.** The authority comes from reading, not from
  credentials or tone. It knows the book better than its critics do.
- **Believes the Creed.** It doesn't hedge. It doesn't present Christianity
  as one lens among several.
- **Not afraid of you.** It states the opponent's case at full strength
  because it isn't threatened by it.
- **An old book.** The text is the host. The voice serves the book, and the
  book serves the reader.
- **Someone he'd like to see become wise.** The reader is a student and a
  soul, not an audience to be won. The voice teaches and it pastors.

### Why a curriculum needs a voice at all

Chesterton gives the reason, in *Heretics*:

> But there are some people, nevertheless—and I am one of them—who think
> that the most practical and important thing about a man is still his view
> of the universe.

A neutral great-books guide pretends it doesn't have a view of the universe,
and so it teaches one anyway without admitting it. This guide says out loud
what it believes, and that makes it more honest, not less.

## Part 2. Four families, and why you need all four

Seventeen influences are too many to hold in your head while writing. They
sort into four families. Each family supplies something the other three
lack, and each goes bad in a particular way when it's left on its own.

| Family | Members | Supplies | Alone, it becomes… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Controversialists** | Wilson, Chesterton, Isker, MacIntyre, Sauvé, Doyle | Conviction, nerve, cheer in combat | **Culture-war sludge**: every book a pretext for the week's outrage |
| **The Imaginers** | Lewis, Tolkien, Pageau, Bunyan | Image, myth, symbol, story | **Symbol-hunting**: patterns floating free of the text |
| **The Preachers** | Spurgeon, Edwards, Bunyan | The heart, application, Christ | **Sentimentality**: warm feelings and no argument |
| **The Reasoners** | Pascal, Leibniz, Kreeft, Adler, Bloom | Argument, clarity, the Great Conversation | **Textbook**: correct, numbered and dead |

Bunyan sits in two families on purpose: he's the proof that image and
preaching can be one act.

**The balancing rule.** Every worldview essay needs a move from each family:

1. one **conviction** stated without hedging (Controversialist)
2. one **image** that carries the argument (Imaginer)
3. one **turn to the reader's heart**, ending at Christ (Preacher)
4. one **argument** a skeptic would have to answer (Reasoner)

If one family is missing, the essay tilts. If one family dominates, the
essay turns into that family's failure mode.

## Part 3. The influences, one by one

Each entry gives the signature move, how it looks in an Omnibus chapter,
and how it goes wrong. The entries for the three living popular voices
(Sauvé, Isker, Doyle) carry an **evidence status**, because the research
behind them hasn't reached primary text yet.

### G. K. Chesterton: the paradox that turns out to be plain sense

**Move.** He takes a truism, turns it upside down, and shows that the
upside-down version is the sane one.

> The madman is not the man who has lost his reason. The madman is the man
> who has lost everything except his reason.
> *Orthodoxy*

> Tradition means giving votes to the most obscure of all classes, our
> ancestors. It is the democracy of the dead.
> *Orthodoxy*

> The Christian ideal has not been tried and found wanting. It has been
> found difficult; and left untried.
> *What's Wrong with the World*

Look at the mechanics. The setup is a common phrase ("lost his reason,"
"tried and found wanting"). The turn changes one word or one structure. The
landing is short. The paradox is never decoration: each one is an argument
compressed until it fits in a sentence.

He also models the concession rule directly:

> Any one setting out to dispute anything ought always to begin by saying
> what he does not dispute.
> *Orthodoxy*

**In the Omnibus.** Use it at most once or twice per essay, at the hinge of
the argument. For example, on Plato's *Republic*: *Plato banished the poets
from his perfect city, and then wrote the most poetic book in philosophy.*

**Goes wrong** when a paradox shows up where a plain sentence would do, or
when three of them come in a row and the reader stops trusting you.

### Douglas Wilson: cheerful combat

**Move.** Polemic that comes from joy, not from anxiety. He takes the
opponent seriously and himself less seriously. He deploys satire as a moral
tool (his *A Serrated Edge* is a defense of exactly that), and holds a
settled confidence that Christ wins history, so there's no need to panic.
There's a covenantal frame throughout: households, generations, the long
game.

**In the Omnibus.** It's the tone of the whole: the reader should feel that
the author is *enjoying* this. Humor is permitted and even expected,
especially at the expense of pride, including Achilles', Agamemnon's and
ours.

**Goes wrong** when it turns into insider combat: jokes that only land if
you know the internet fight, or contempt where there should be cheer.

### Jonathan Pageau: symbolic sight

**Move.** He reads the world as a pattern that repeats at every scale:
heaven and earth, center and margin, head and body, order and chaos, the
city and the wilderness beyond it. A story's layout is itself meaning. What
sits at the center and what gets pushed to the edge tell you what a culture
worships.

**In the Omnibus.** It's the key to scenes the essays might otherwise
skip. The shield of Achilles is a cosmos: the heavens at the center, then
cities of peace and war, fields and dances, the river Ocean at the rim. The
tabernacle, Dante's rose, and the city plan in the *Republic* all reward
this kind of reading.

**Goes wrong** when the pattern is imposed instead of found. The test: can
you point to the line in the text?

### C. S. Lewis: lucid argument, carried by one homely picture

**Move.** He explains an abstract claim with a household analogy so clear
you can't unsee it. He diagnoses "chronological snobbery," the assumption
that whatever is newer must be truer. And he argues for reading old books as
correctives to the blind spots of our own age (his famous introduction to
Athanasius, which is not PD).

**In the Omnibus.** It's the default method of the worldview essay: one
claim, one picture, carried all the way through. It's also the reason the
curriculum exists at all.

**Goes wrong** when you try to rewrite his famous lines, which always come
out worse.

### J. R. R. Tolkien: myth as a true thing

**Move.** From his essay *On Fairy-Stories*, which is not PD: humans
*sub-create* because they are made in the image of a Maker, and the best
stories turn on a *eucatastrophe*, the sudden joyous turn that gives a
glimpse of the Gospel, which is the true myth. Add to that a reverence for
languages, roots and old names.

**In the Omnibus.** It's the frame for every pagan myth on the list. Myths
are neither lies to debunk nor truths to baptize wholesale. They're
reachings, and some reach further than others. Priam's night visit is
almost a eucatastrophe, and knowing why it stops short is the lesson.

**Goes wrong** as elvish pastiche and archaism.

### Brian Sauvé: the household as the first Christendom

**Evidence status (2026-09-24).** Search-engine summaries only. Three
research passes tried to read transcripts, sermons and articles, and this
environment's network policy blocked every page. Nothing below is drawn
from his own sentences yet. Upgrade this entry from primary text before
relying on it.

**Who.** Pastor of Refuge Church, Ogden, Utah; president of New
Christendom Press; co-founder of a classical Christian school. His podcasts
include *The King's Hall*, *Haunted Cosmos* (with Ben Garrett, on the
supernatural), *Bright Hearth* (with his wife, on the productive household)
and *Stump the Pastors*.

**Themes the sources support.**
- Christian culture-building, framed through history: a 2024 conference
  talk on King Alfred and "Christian boroughs."
- The household as a productive unit, not just a place to sleep.
- Fathers as teachers: nightly family worship, catechesis, working through
  Proverbs with a son.
- Psalm-singing, and classical Christian schooling.
- An open, unembarrassed belief in the supernatural. The spiritual world
  is real, not a metaphor.

**Move (inferred from those themes).** Doctrine gets lived out first at
the table, and a civilization is rebuilt one household and one town at a
time. The long game is played by fathers.

**In the Omnibus.**
- It shapes the Application section: what a reader does with a book at
  home, not only what he thinks of it.
- It supplies historical exemplars of builders: Alfred (vol. II), Benedict,
  Boniface.
- It explains why the Recitation section exists: memory work is family
  catechesis carried into the schoolroom.
- Hector at his gate with his wife and son is the most Sauvé moment in
  Homer.

**Not yet known.** How he argues, his humor, his pacing, and how he treats
opponents. The searches also surfaced critical press coverage, unread.

### C. H. Spurgeon: warmth, earthy images, and always Christ

**Move.** Images from the farmyard and the kitchen. He speaks directly to
the reader ("you"). Every text is made to walk to the cross.

**In the Omnibus.** Every worldview essay ends at Christ, and it gets there
by honest roads, not by a sudden swerve in the last paragraph.

**Goes wrong** as Victorian padding and exclamation marks.

### Jonathan Edwards: the beauty of God, and the heart's loves

**Move.** For Edwards, true religion lives in the *affections*, the heart's
settled loves and hatreds, not in bare opinion. God's beauty ("excellency")
is the center of everything. This is a paraphrase of the thesis of
*Religious Affections*. Quote it only once canon-corpus's `edwards-affections`
is built and the wording can be checked against it.

**In the Omnibus.** It changes the goal of the questions. We aren't only
asking what the student thinks of Achilles. We're asking what the student
has come to *love*. The Application section works on the affections.

**Goes wrong** as density for its own sake.

### Blaise Pascal: greatness and wretchedness held together

**Move.** The fragment. Man's dignity and misery are set side by side, and
neither is allowed to cancel the other.

> Man is but a reed, the most feeble thing in nature; but he is a thinking
> reed.
> *Pensées* 347

> The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know.
> *Pensées* 277

> The eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me.
> *Pensées* 206

And the wager, which is honesty about stakes:

> If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing. Wager, then,
> without hesitation that He is.
> *Pensées* (the wager fragment)

**In the Omnibus.** It's the anthropology of every chapter. Homer's
heroes are thinking reeds: noble because they know they die, wretched
because they have nothing else to know.

**Goes wrong** when an aphorism stands in for an argument that was never
made.

### G. W. Leibniz: sufficient reason and the problem of evil at full strength

**Move.** Nothing happens without a sufficient reason. And he takes the
problem of evil head on (in the *Theodicy*, which has no PD English
translation) instead of ducking it.

**In the Omnibus.** It covers the books where the question is *why*:
Job, the *Oresteia*, Boethius, Achilles' two urns. The essay has to state
the objection the way the sufferer would.

**Goes wrong** as system-building nobody asked for.

### Peter Kreeft: Socratic clarity

**Move.** Define the terms, number the arguments, answer the objection in
its best form. Put it in dialogue when possible.

**In the Omnibus.** The questions are his domain. Each session climbs a
ladder: comprehension, then interpretation, then evaluation, then the heart.
The Socratic debate prompt comes from him.

**Goes wrong** when the essay turns into a handout.

### Mortimer Adler: the Great Conversation

**Move.** The great books are one long conversation about a set of great
ideas: justice, love, God, the soul, virtue, war, fate. Reading is active
work. You come to terms with the author and state his propositions *before*
you judge them (*How to Read a Book*).

**In the Omnibus.** Two concrete rules come from him:
- Every chapter names which great ideas it joins (see Part 6).
- No book is critiqued until it has been stated fairly, which is Adler's
  rule and Chesterton's rule combined.

**Goes wrong** as a neutral referee's stance. Adler lays out the
conversation; we take a side in it.

### Harold Bloom: the book is great, not a symptom

**Move.** He defended the canon on the grounds of aesthetic greatness,
against critics who read every book only as an expression of power. He read
slowly, aloud, and from memory. He saw strong writers wrestling with the
writers who came before them.

**In the Omnibus.** It protects against the chronic disease of Christian
curricula: reading a pagan book only to refute it. Before you judge Homer,
show the student why Homer is *magnificent*: the similes, the speed, the
pity. A student who hasn't felt the beauty of the *Iliad* hasn't earned the
right to critique it.

**Goes wrong** as his own religious views, which were gnostic and
unorthodox. He's an ally on what to read and not on what the books finally
mean.

### Andrew Isker: the axe and the oak

**Evidence status (2026-09-24).** Search-engine summaries only. Three
research passes tried to read transcripts, sermons, book excerpts and articles, and this
environment's network policy blocked every page. Nothing below is drawn
from his own sentences yet. Upgrade this entry from primary text before
relying on it.

**Who.** A Reformed pastor, formerly in Waseca, Minnesota, now planting a
church in Tennessee. He wrote *The Boniface Option* (New Christendom Press,
2023) and co-wrote *Christian Nationalism: A Biblical Guide for Taking
Dominion and Discipling Nations* (2022). He co-hosts the *Contra Mundum*
podcast.

**The central image.** Boniface, the eighth-century missionary, cut down
Donar's Oak, the sacred tree of the Germanic tribes. The god did not
strike him, and the tribes' faith in the idol broke. Isker sets this
"Boniface Option" (offense, public confrontation of idols) against Rod
Dreher's *Benedict Option*, which he reads as withdrawal. Reviewers connect
it to Elijah and the prophets of Baal. Whether Isker makes that link
himself isn't confirmed.

**Moves the reviews describe.**
- Argument by historical exemplar: missionary saints, Christendom, the
  older liturgies treated as living wisdom to recover, not dead custom.
- Coined names for the present age ("Trashworld" is the central one),
  plus slang epithets for opponents.
- Offense over retreat, joined to practical counsel about fathers and
  local communities.
- Opponents are named and mocked rather than answered point by point. A
  sympathetic critic, Rod Dreher, called the writing sharp but
  undisciplined and warned that contempt for evil can slide into contempt
  for people. Friendly reviewers (Doug Wilson among them) read the same
  tone as unapologetic confidence.

**Take.**
- The **axe and the oak**. It's a perfect Omnibus image: the idol is
  shown to be powerless by someone walking up and testing it. That's what
  a worldview essay does to a false idea, and it works for Marx,
  Nietzsche, and the gods of Homer.
- **Nerve**: say plainly that some ideas on the syllabus are false and
  destructive, and show why.
- **Church history as a source of courage.** Boniface belongs in vol. V.

**Leave.**
- The slang and epithets. They date a book within five years and model
  mockery for teenagers.
- Mockery in the place of argument. The Omnibus rule is that the author
  gets his strongest case first. Boniface didn't mock the oak. He cut it
  down in front of everyone, and the chopping was the argument.
- **Attribution risk.** News coverage reports his public criticism of Jews
  and other non-Christians. The Omnibus should borrow the stance and the
  image, not the name, and never cite him as an authority in a chapter.

### Auron MacIntyre: how power actually works

**Move.** Political realism: who actually rules, how elites and
institutions hold power, how managerial systems grow. *The Total State* is
his book-length argument.

**In the Omnibus.** It's useful for the political books: Thucydides'
Melian dialogue, *The Prince*, *Leviathan*, the *Federalist*, Tocqueville.
It helps students see the machinery beneath the rhetoric.

**Goes wrong** as current-events commentary. Name no living politicians
and no current parties. Books outlast news cycles, and the curriculum has
to as well.

### John Doyle: the young man's plain speech

**Evidence status (2026-09-24).** Search-engine summaries only. Three
research passes tried to read transcripts, sermons and articles, and this
environment's network policy blocked every page. Nothing below is drawn
from his own sentences yet. Upgrade this entry from primary text before
relying on it.

**Who.** An American commentator, born 1999. He started the YouTube show
*Heck Off, Commie!* and now hosts *The John Doyle Show* on BlazeTV, where
he's a colleague of Auron MacIntyre (the two have debated whether America
should "embrace empire"). 2022 sources describe him as a practicing
Catholic. **No conversion story was found.** If you remember one, send the
source.

**Themes the sources support.**
- Masculinity and a "warrior ethos."
- Anti-left culture commentary and America First politics.
- Long video essays that read pop culture closely. There's a tongue-in-cheek
  "Neo-Gastonism" series, built on the villain of Disney's *Beauty and the
  Beast*.
- Reading media and institutional choices as symbolic acts, for example
  calling a viral military video a "humiliation ritual."

**Correction to the first draft of this guide.** The earlier entry called
Doyle's theme "beauty, rootedness, a masculine imagination." Nothing the
research found supports *beauty* or *rootedness*. That was my impression,
not his record. If those themes are what drew you to him, a video or
episode would confirm them.

**Take.**
- **Plain, confident address to young people**, especially young men, with
  no condescension.
- **Pop culture as a door.** A student who has seen *Beauty and the Beast*
  already knows Gaston is an Achilles gone to seed. Use what they know to
  reach what they don't, in the Openings and the Cultural Analysis
  questions.
- **Admiring heroes out loud.** Achilles is magnificent, and saying so
  isn't naive.

**Leave.**
- Insult humor. It's built for reaction videos, not for books that will be
  read in 2050.
- Reading every institution as a hostile ritual. It trains suspicion where
  the curriculum needs attention.
- Empire and power talk tuned to the news cycle. MacIntyre's entry covers
  power, and at the level of the political books.

### John Bunyan: allegory in plain English

**Move.** Spiritual realities given homely names and places: the Slough of
Despond, Vanity Fair, Giant Despair, Mr. Worldly Wiseman. The Christian
life has the shape of a journey.

**In the Omnibus.** It covers naming, for the student. Teach them to name
what they see in a book the way Bunyan would. Achilles in book 9 is sitting
in Doubting Castle.

**Goes wrong** as archaism for its own sake.

## Part 4. The toolkit, sentence by sentence

### 4.1 Build and land

A long sentence that accumulates clause by clause toward a peak, then a
short flat sentence that lands it. Chesterton does it in three steps:

> The poet only asks to get his head into the heavens. It is the logician
> who seeks to get the heavens into his head. And it is his head that
> splits.
> *Orthodoxy*

The last sentence is the shortest, and the key stressed word comes last.

**Rule:** end paragraphs on a stress, not a qualification. *"And it is his
head that splits,"* not *"which may, in some cases, lead to difficulty."*

### 4.2 The concession at full strength

Structure: **their case → in their words → better than they'd make it →
the turn.**

The turn usually comes from inside their own case. The Iliad essay's best
move is its best example: Achilles' own words about the two fates are the
case for glory, and his own weeping in book 24 is the case against it.

**Signal phrases** (use them sparingly): *"Start with the strongest case
for…"* / *"That is not a savage's code."* / *"Now look at what it actually
produces."*

### 4.3 One running image

Choose the image *before* drafting. It should be:
- **physical**: something you could touch (hands, a shield, a gate, a
  keel)
- **from the text**: found in the book, not brought in from outside
- **able to carry the turn**: it has to mean one thing at the start and
  something transformed at the end

The Iliad essay: *hands.* Killing hands, then kissed hands, then pierced
hands.

### 4.4 The Christological landing

Every essay ends at Christ, but the road there has to be honest. There are
three legitimate roads:
1. **Fulfillment**: the book reaches for something the Gospel supplies
   (Priam's ransom, John 19:38).
2. **Contrast**: the book's answer fails where the Gospel's holds
   (Jove's urns against Job's God).
3. **Judgment**: the book's own logic, followed to the end, condemns it
   (Achilles' glory against Phil. 2:7).

**Forbidden road:** the swerve. That's the last paragraph that suddenly
mentions Jesus with no road leading there.

### 4.5 The Socratic ladder (questions)

Order: **what happened → what it means → whether it's true → what you
love.** Each question should be answerable only from the page, except
Application, which is answerable only from the student's life.

Write questions in plain, clean English. There are no flourishes in
questions. The voice lives in the essay; the questions are tools.

### 4.6 The second-person turn

In the voice's best moments, it looks up from the book and at the reader:
*"Whose hands are you refusing to take?"* Use it once per essay, near the
end, and in the Application questions. More than once and it turns into a
sermon.

### 4.7 Diction

- **Anglo-Saxon over Latinate** whenever the meaning survives: *hands* not
  *extremities*, *kill* not *eliminate*, *brave* not *courageous* (unless
  you need the rhythm).
- **Concrete nouns, strong verbs.** Cut adverbs.
- **Contractions** are fine in openings and essays. Skip them in questions
  and the teacher's key.
- **The KJV is the ear.** When unsure how a sentence should sound, read a
  KJV verse aloud and match its weight:
  > the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong
  > `kjv:Eccl.9.11`

### 4.8 The ban list

Cut on sight. These are the tells of writing that isn't thinking:

- *arguably, in many ways, it is important to note, it's worth noting,
  interestingly, notably*
- *delve, tapestry, navigate (for anything that isn't a ship), multifaceted,
  nuanced, rich (for texts), resonate, journey (except Bunyan's)*
- *In conclusion. Ultimately. At the end of the day.*
- *Not only X but also Y* more than once per chapter
- Triplets used as rhythm with nothing in them
- *"most X ever written"* superlatives. The Iliad draft currently says
  *"the most Christian moment in any pagan book."* Earn it or cut it.

## Part 6. Before and after

Each pair takes the same content in two versions: flat, then in the voice.

**Plato's cave**

> *Flat:* Plato's allegory of the cave illustrates his theory that the
> physical world is a shadow of a higher reality of Forms, and that
> education involves turning the soul toward the truth.

> *Voice:* Plato chained his readers in a cave, lit a fire behind them,
> and made them watch shadows on a wall for a lifetime. Then he dragged one
> of them up into the sun, and let the sun hurt. Every teacher who has
> watched a student's eyes water at a hard truth has stood at the mouth of
> that cave. Plato was right that the light hurts. He didn't know that the
> Light would come down into the cave.

**Virgil's Aeneas**

> *Flat:* Aeneas represents the Roman virtue of pietas, and he sacrifices
> his personal desires for the sake of his duty to found Rome.

> *Voice:* Aeneas carries his father on his back out of burning Troy and
> leads his son by the hand. That picture is Rome: the past on your
> shoulders, the future at your side, and no hand left free for yourself.
> It is magnificent, and it is exhausting, and Virgil knew both.

**Transitional sentence**

> *Flat:* It is important to note that Hector is also a complex character
> worth considering.

> *Voice:* Now look at Hector.

## Part 7. The Great Ideas tags (the Adler rule)

Every chapter's front matter should carry an `ideas:` list drawn from a
fixed vocabulary, so the six volumes can be searched as one conversation.
The proposed starting vocabulary is deliberately short:

`God · Fate & Providence · Justice · Law · Love · Friendship · Honor &
Glory · Courage · Pride & Humility · Anger · Death · Immortality · The Soul
· Virtue · Sin · Grace · Beauty · Truth · War · Peace · The City · Kingship
& Tyranny · Family & Household · Man & Woman · Nature · Art & Poetry ·
Wisdom · Knowledge · Time & History · Covenant · Sacrifice · Hospitality`

For the Iliad: `Anger · Honor & Glory · Death · Fate & Providence · War ·
Friendship · Family & Household · Hospitality · Pity` (add *Pity* to the
vocabulary, or fold it into *Love*).
