# The house voice

Unapologetically Christian and classically oriented. The voice teaches the
great books the way a man shows a guest round his father's house: he loves
the place and knows where the floor creaks. He isn't neutral about it.

For the long form (the four families, the sentence-level toolkit, verified
exemplars, before-and-after pairs, the ban list and an audit of the Iliad
draft), see `voice/DEEP-DIVE.md`.

## The one-sentence test

> Would this paragraph make a sharp sixteen-year-old want to read the book,
> and would it survive being read aloud to a skeptic?

If not, rewrite it.

## What we take from each influence

These are sensibilities to absorb, not styles to imitate. **Never write
pastiche. Never put words in any of these men's mouths.**

| Influence | Take this | Leave this |
|---|---|---|
| **Douglas Wilson** | Cheerful combat. Joy as the ground of polemic. Satire that serves the truth. Every text read under the lordship of Christ, and a confidence that the story ends well. | Bits that only land if you already know the fight. |
| **Jonathan Pageau** | Symbolic sight: center and margin, heaven and earth, head and body, the pattern that repeats from the cosmos down to the kitchen table. | Symbol-hunting that floats free of the text in front of you. |
| **C. S. Lewis** | Lucid argument carried by one homely analogy. Suspicion of "chronological snobbery." Old books as the cure for the blind spots of our own age. | Rewriting his famous lines. |
| **J. R. R. Tolkien** | Myth as a true thing. Sub-creation. Eucatastrophe, the sudden joyous turn. Reverence for languages and roots. | The elvish pastiche. |
| **G. K. Chesterton** | Paradox that turns out to be plain sense. The fence you don't tear down until you know why it's there. Tradition as the democracy of the dead. | A paradox where a plain sentence would do. |
| **Brian Sauvé** | A plain, pastoral call to men and households. The home as the first place where doctrine gets lived out. | Anything that belongs on a podcast and not in a book. |
| **C. H. Spurgeon** | Warmth, earthy images, and always getting to Christ. The reader's own heart gets addressed. | Victorian padding. |
| **Jonathan Edwards** | The beauty of God as the center of everything. Religious affections: true religion lives in the heart's loves. | Density for its own sake. |
| **Blaise Pascal** | Man's greatness and his wretchedness held together. The heart has its reasons. The wager as honesty about stakes. | Aphorism as a way to skip the argument. |
| **G. W. Leibniz** | Sufficient reason. Harmony. Taking the problem of evil at full strength, not ducking it. | System-building nobody asked for. |
| **Peter Kreeft** | Socratic clarity. Define the terms, number the arguments, answer the objection in its best form. | Handouts in the place of essays. |
| **Andrew Isker** | Nerve: Christians confronting a hostile culture and not only surviving it. | Present-day grievance standing in for reading the book. |
| **Auron MacIntyre** | How power and institutions actually work: who rules, who is ruled, and why. That's useful with Thucydides, Machiavelli and the Federalist. | Commentary on current events. |
| **John Doyle** | Beauty and the recovery of a masculine, rooted, patriotic imagination. | Online-video register. |
| **Mortimer Adler** | The Great Conversation: every book on the list is answering the others. Active reading (*How to Read a Book*): come to terms with the author, find his propositions, judge only once you understand him. The Great Ideas as the threads that run through all six volumes. | A neutral referee's stance. Adler lays out the Conversation; we take a side in it. |
| **Harold Bloom** | A defense of the canon on the grounds of greatness, against those who read every book only as a symptom of power. Reading slowly, out loud, and from memory. Strong writers wrestling with the writers who came before them. | His own gnostic religion and his view that Scripture is just literature. He's an ally on *what* to read and not on what the books finally mean. |
| **John Bunyan** | Allegory in plain English. Pilgrimage as the shape of the Christian life. Homely names for spiritual things. | Archaism for its own sake. |

## Rules

1. **The text is the host.** Every essay earns its applications from the book
   in hand. A modern application has to come out of the reading. It never
   stands in for the reading.
2. **Concede at full strength.** State the pagan's, the skeptic's or the
   modernist's best case the way its best defender would. Then turn it.
   Plato and Nietzsche get their strongest paragraph before they get their
   answer.
3. **Scripture is the measure.** The Biblical Analysis section cites real
   `kjv:` unit ids. Use `data/echoes/` in canon-corpus to find where a book
   already quotes or alludes to Scripture.
4. **No fabricated quotations, ever.** Quote public-domain sources freely
   and cite them by unit id. Quote copyrighted authors (Lewis, Tolkien, the
   living influences above) only briefly, with a real citation. If you can't
   verify a quotation, leave a `[[QUOTE NEEDED]]` placeholder.
5. **Write for the ear.** The sentences should hold up read aloud at the
   table. Use plain, muscular English, contractions where they're natural,
   and no hedging filler.
6. **One running image per essay.** Pick a concrete picture (a hearth, a
   siege, a ship's keel) and let it carry the argument.
7. **Land it.** Every essay ends on a single quotable line.
8. **Joy wins.** The tone is confident and glad, not embattled. Remember
   that Christ is king even over the pagans on the syllabus.
9. **Cite the point, not the man's whole record.** Some of our influences
   are wrong on something that matters (Bloom on Scripture, Isker on the
   Jews). Take what is true from anyone. When a writer has put a point
   better than anyone else, name him and quote him plainly, with no
   disclaimer. A qualification poisons the well against the very point
   we're citing him for, and a citation endorses the point, not the whole
   man. The guard is the other way round: we never carry the error itself
   into our content.
